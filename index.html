Grand Project · 2027–2031

HUMAN-UKRA human-centered perspective on the consequences of war in Ukraine

How has the war in Ukraine impacted the lives of Ukrainian people, and what are the lessons for the Baltic Sea Region and Eastern Europe?

A five-year, interdisciplinary research project that follows a representative panel of about 5,000 Ukrainians across the country through the war and examines how Russia’s full-scale invasion has affected their lives across six domains: economics, politics, the environment, well-being, information and social relations. Survey responses are linked to independent data on the war: geocoded records of attacks, explosion data, and data on environmental damage.

01 Why this matters

Beyond the headlines

Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine has caused immense human suffering, environmental degradation, economic disruption, social fragmentation, rapid political shifts and information warfare. While geopolitical and military developments dominate the headlines, HUMAN-UKR redirects attention to ordinary Ukrainians: those enduring the daily realities of war, who will ultimately play the central role in post-war reconstruction.

  • Six domains, studied together. The consequences of the war for economics, politics, the environment, well-being, information and social relations are examined in one integrated design, because people experience them simultaneously and disruptions in one domain affect the others.
  • Open questions. Research on armed conflict offers a strong foundation, but three questions remain open: how a large-scale, conventional interstate war affects people’s lives across domains, and how those effects interact; how persistent violence, war fatigue and major events shape attitudes and behaviour over time; and how the form and intensity of violence make a difference.
  • Lessons for the region. The war’s effects extend across the Baltic Sea Region and Eastern Europe. While governments recalibrate security policies, less is known about how citizens themselves endure and adapt to war. Documenting this systematically provides lessons for regional preparedness.

“The overarching purpose of HUMAN-UKR is to deliver a comprehensive, human-centered understanding of how the war in Ukraine has impacted the lives of citizens and to assess the broader implications for the Baltic Sea Region and Eastern Europe. The unprecedented character of the war in Ukraine offers a historical opportunity to advance core social science debates and inform policies to support the affected people.”

02 Seven questions

What the project asks

The overarching question is addressed through seven sub-questions, each assigned to a work package: six domains of life, and a seventh that draws lessons for the region from the other six.

Economic games embedded in the surveys measure willingness to cooperate, together with basic economic preferences, such as preferences for time and risk, and pro-sociality, and trace how these change or remain stable over the course of the war.

Research question (WP1). How does a major war shape economic and social preferences for cooperation?

Lead: Eric Skoog, Södertörn University

Resistance motivations, democratic attitudes and leader preferences are tracked over five years with validated survey measures. Self-reported experiences of violence are linked to independent records of attack events and explosion data, so that the effects of different types and intensities of violence can be analysed over time.

Research question (WP2). How does persistent civilian victimization affect resistance motivations, support for democracy, and leader preferences?

Lead: Henrikas Bartusevicius, PRIO

High-resolution data at district or municipal level on land degradation, water contamination and deforestation are linked to survey measures of perceived degradation, trust in institutions, civic engagement and adaptation behaviour, while administrative and policy records document how governance responds.

Research question (WP3). How does war-induced environmental degradation affect people’s interaction within and between local communities and governance structures?

Lead: [Name], Södertörn University

Well-being is measured in its cognitive, affective and eudaimonic dimensions in both the main panel survey and the Life in War Survey, whose baseline was collected two weeks before the 2022 full-scale invasion, so that well-being before and during the war can be compared. The surveys also cover war-related insecurity and the ways people cope.

Research question (WP4). How does a major war impact different aspects of subjective well-being?

Lead: Ruslana Moskotina, Kyiv School of Economics

The war in Ukraine has been described as the first full-blown “social media war”. The longitudinal surveys map how people consume information, which sources they trust and how they evaluate what they encounter, and how these patterns change over the course of the war.

Research question (WP5). How does a major war shape information consumption and susceptibility to misinformation?

Lead: [Name], BI Norwegian Business School

The main panel survey tracks community relations and attitudes towards veterans across the country over five years. In addition, two rounds of in-depth interviews with around 50 veteran families across diverse regions provide longitudinal evidence on social reintegration, and a survey of around 800 employers examines economic reintegration and veterans’ employment prospects.

Research question (WP6). How does a major war influence community relations, particularly the social and economic reintegration of veterans?

Lead: Júlia Palik, PRIO

Two cross-work-package workshops, mid-project and at the end, identify themes that cut across the findings. A policy lab with Ukrainian and international practitioners turns them into comparative lessons for neighbouring countries on how prolonged exposure to violence shapes democratic attitudes, economic behaviour, well-being, coping strategies and trust.

Research question (WP7). What strategies can effectively address the impacts documented in WP1–WP6 to enhance resilience and recovery in Ukraine, and preparedness in the Baltic Sea Region and Eastern Europe?

Lead: all team members contribute

03 How the project works

A multi-method design

HUMAN-UKR combines longitudinal surveys, in-depth interviews, randomised experiments, spatial analysis, and conflict event and explosion data within a single analytical framework, studying the war in real time rather than through retrospective accounts.

  1. Longitudinal surveys

    A replenished panel of about 5,000 Ukrainians, representative of the country, is surveyed every three months over five years, 18 waves in all, producing nearly 100,000 individual observations across all six domains. In parallel, the Life in War Survey runs twice a year with roughly 1,000 respondents per wave, partly following participants first surveyed two weeks before the 2022 full-scale invasion. Both surveys include randomised experiments, and more than 100 in-depth interviews, with repeated sessions for 50 veteran families, add qualitative depth.

  2. Linked to place

    Respondents’ approximate locations are recorded at each wave and matched in space and time to independent data: geocoded military events, environmental and infrastructural damage, and explosion data that do not depend on media reporting. Around 100 currently accessible districts (raions) serve as the higher-level units in multilevel analyses.

  3. Followed over time

    Because the same people are surveyed repeatedly, the project can track how attitudes and behaviour evolve through the war, how they respond to major military or political events between waves, and how wartime measures compare with the pre-invasion baseline. Interrupted time-series analysis and multilevel models link self-reports to objectively measured exposure to violence.

  4. Three measures of violence

    Exposure to violence is measured in three independent ways: what respondents report, geocoded records of attack events, and explosion data. Linking them shows how objectively measured violence aligns with people’s own perceptions of it, and how both affect the outcomes studied.

05 Outputs

What the project will produce

Findings are communicated within the research disciplines and to society at large, in Ukraine and across the region.

  • Journal articles. Each work package aims for three articles in general science and leading disciplinary journals, with one article per work package proposed for a special issue.
  • An edited volume synthesising the findings into policy recommendations, with contributions from scholars in Ukraine, the Baltic Sea Region and Eastern Europe.
  • Policy briefs and rapid-response memos from each work package, in Ukrainian and English, and workshops with practitioners.
  • Public engagement. Blog posts, podcast episodes and public lectures, with all popular articles translated into Ukrainian.
  • Open data. Anonymised datasets, survey instruments, interview guides and code deposited in an open repository, plus an interactive website where users can explore the data, run basic analyses and create visualisations.
